## How to read the columns

**Type** — *diagnostic* (a claim about how things are), *policy* (a claim about what should be done), *value* (a claim about what matters).

**Expected split** — a prediction, recorded before the fact so it can be compared against results. *Consensus* = expect >75% agreement; *lean* = expect 55–75%; *divisive* = expect a real split; *unknown* = no basis for a guess. Sets where everything is marked consensus are badly built.

## Design notes

**Every set contains positions the white paper argues against.** These are written in their strongest sympathetic form, not as the paper's characterisation of them. Sets B and D carry the most of these, since the paper's rejected postures (defensive closure, passive acceptance) and its most contested proposal (interface agency) live there.

**Provenance disclosure is an open decision.** The Klimarat finding is that a visible institutional prefix biased voting. Presenting these as *the white paper's proposals* will plausibly pull votes toward deference. Against that, the paper's own legitimacy argument favours disclosure. Recommendation: disclose the source, but frame the statements as "positions drawn from the paper, including ones it rejects" rather than "the paper's proposals" — which is accurate and blunts the deference effect.

**Sharpness convention.** Sharp means the claim is contested, not that the wording is hot. Statement G-09 ("Solutions built for Global Majority communities will also serve Global North communities, while the reverse does not hold") is deliberately provocative in substance and deliberately flat in tone. That combination is the target throughout.

**Running order.** If only some sets run, the highest-value three are **B** (forces a posture choice), **D** (the mission's most contested claim), and **F** (closest to editors, where the institutional and volunteer voices are furthest apart). Set A is the natural opener since it tests the premises the other seven rest on.
